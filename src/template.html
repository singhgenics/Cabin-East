From a Calcutta street to a Goa beach

How a wok, a lot of chilli and one big idea about crunch crossed India, and why we cook it the way we do.

  1. 1770s A sailor, a sugar mill and a village called Achipur

    It starts on the Hooghly river. A Chinese sailor the English called Atchew arrived from Guangdong, set up a sugar plantation outside Calcutta and brought more men from home to work it. The village is still called Achipur. Nobody was thinking about dinner yet.

  2. Mornings at Tiretta Bazaar, nights in Tangra

    Calcutta’s first Chinatown grew around Tiretta Bazaar, where steam still rises off the breakfast stalls at dawn. Many Chinese families worked in leather, and when the tanneries moved to the eastern edge of the city, a second Chinatown grew up around them in Tangra. The kitchens followed the work.

  3. Cooking for Indian tongues

    Chinese cooks in Calcutta found their neighbours wanted more: crispier, hotter, louder. Garlic went in by the fistful, green chillies followed, soy met ginger and the kind of heat the city already loved. One cook remembers chilli chicken starting as fried chicken with onions and garlic, with the chillies coming later. It wasn’t quite Chinese and it wasn’t quite Bengali. It was something new.

  4. Where we got our name

    Calcutta’s old cafes had cabins: wooden booths with curtains or swing doors, where a family, a pair of friends or a pair of sweethearts could talk for hours over something fried. We borrowed the name, not the curtains. Cabin East is the same idea: a small, warm place with good food in front of you and no fuss.

  5. 1975 Mumbai adds a dish, and India takes the rest

    As the food travelled, it changed. In Mumbai in 1975, a chef named Nelson Wang is credited with inventing Chicken Manchurian at the Cricket Club of India, and the sauce spread faster than the story. Soon every city had carts, canteens and wedding buffets built around noodles, Manchurian and fried rice.

  6. Today A full flame, a short walk from the Arabian Sea

    Now it has reached Betalbatim. We cook it the way the street does, on a roaring flame, and we let Goa in: chouriço in the siu mai, kokum in the gose, craft beer from up the coast. Pull up a stool. Order something hot.
